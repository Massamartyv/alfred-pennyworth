# Materia Medica

Seed herbal reference for a root-cause practice. Adaptogens, nervines, bitters, anti-inflammatories, immune, sleep, digestive and metabolic material. For each botanical: primary actions, an honest evidence grade, typical preparations and the interactions and cautions that matter.

---

## The rule that governs this file

This file never overrides the interaction check. It supports it.

Nothing here is a clearance. The cautions listed under each botanical are the ones that recur most often in the literature, not a complete accounting, and a botanical with a short caution line is not therefore safe for a given person on a given medication list. The check demanded by the charter runs against the actual current medications, the actual current supplements and the actual current conditions, every time, using a current interaction resource. A recommendation made from this file alone is a recommendation made without the check.

Dosing is confirmed against a current source before any recommendation leaves the mouth. The ranges below are orientation – they say roughly what part of the map a dose lives in and nothing more. Preparations differ in strength by an order of magnitude between a whole-herb powder, a tincture at a stated ratio and a standardised extract quoted to a marker compound, and quoting a number from memory across those forms is how people get hurt. Cite the dose or do not give it.

Two further standing rules.

Grades are applied honestly, using the four grades of the charter – Strong, Moderate, Traditional use, Theoretical. A grade here describes the evidence for a specific claim, not for the plant in general. Most botanicals below carry different grades for different indications, and where they do, both are named. Traditional use is a real grade and is used without embarrassment; it is simply never dressed as trial evidence.

Practitioner-grade material only, sourced through Fullscript. Botanical supply is poorly regulated, adulteration is common in several of the species below and identity matters more than dose in a substituted product.

## Domain Constants

Every botanical recommendation is charted with its evidence grade, its preparation and dose, the source of that dose, the ladder rung it sits on and a follow-up date. The charter treats a botanical exactly as it treats a drug on those four counts, because pharmacologically it is one.

## Context

## Adaptogens

### Ashwagandha – Withania somnifera

- **Actions:** adaptogen, anxiolytic, mildly sedating, thyroid-supportive, androgen-supportive
- **Evidence:** Moderate for perceived stress, anxiety and sleep quality across several small randomised controlled trials of eight to twelve weeks. Moderate at best and inconsistent for testosterone and strength. Traditional use for the broader rasayana tonic claims.
- **Preparations:** standardised root extract, commonly 300 to 600 mg daily of a withanolide-standardised product; whole root powder at higher gram-level doses in the Ayurvedic tradition
- **Cautions:** raises thyroid hormone output and can tip a euthyroid person on replacement toward excess. Immunostimulating, so caution in autoimmune disease. Additive with sedatives, benzodiazepines and alcohol. Contraindicated in pregnancy. Rare idiosyncratic hepatotoxicity has been reported, enough to warrant stopping on any unexplained jaundice or right upper quadrant pain. A nightshade, which matters for those who react to the family.

### Rhodiola – Rhodiola rosea

- **Actions:** adaptogen, anti-fatigue, mildly stimulating, cognitive-supportive under stress
- **Evidence:** Moderate for fatigue and stress-related exhaustion, with several trials of modest quality and short duration. Traditional use for altitude tolerance and endurance.
- **Preparations:** root extract standardised to rosavins and salidroside, commonly 200 to 400 mg daily, taken in the morning
- **Cautions:** activating, so evening dosing costs sleep. Caution in bipolar disorder, where activating adaptogens can precipitate elevation. Theoretical monoamine oxidase interaction warrants caution alongside serotonergic drugs. Avoid stacking with high stimulant load.

### Eleuthero – Eleutherococcus senticosus

- **Actions:** adaptogen, endurance-supportive, mildly immune-modulating
- **Evidence:** Traditional use, with a large Soviet-era literature that does not meet modern standards. Theoretical for most performance claims.
- **Preparations:** root decoction or standardised extract, commonly 300 to 1,200 mg daily of dried root equivalent
- **Cautions:** may raise blood pressure in some. Interferes with digoxin assays and can produce a falsely elevated reading. Stimulating enough to disturb sleep if taken late.

### Asian ginseng – Panax ginseng

- **Actions:** adaptogen, cognitive and vitality tonic, erectile-supportive, glycaemic-modulating
- **Evidence:** Moderate for cognitive performance, fatigue and erectile function across several small trials. Traditional use for the classical tonic indications, which is by far the larger body of practice.
- **Preparations:** standardised root extract quoted to ginsenoside content, commonly 200 to 400 mg daily, cycled rather than continuous
- **Cautions:** lowers blood glucose, so additive with hypoglycaemic drugs. Reduces warfarin effect in reported cases. Stimulating, with insomnia and irritability at higher doses. Caution in hormone-sensitive conditions and in uncontrolled hypertension.

### Holy basil – Ocimum tenuiflorum

- **Actions:** adaptogen, anxiolytic, glycaemic-modulating, mildly anti-inflammatory
- **Evidence:** Traditional use, extensive across Ayurveda. Moderate at the edge for stress and metabolic markers on the strength of a handful of small trials.
- **Preparations:** leaf infusion as tulsi tea or leaf extract commonly 300 to 600 mg daily
- **Cautions:** mild antiplatelet activity, so caution with anticoagulants and before surgery. Lowers blood glucose. Animal work suggests possible antifertility effect, which is worth naming where conception is being attempted.

### Cordyceps – Cordyceps militaris

- **Actions:** adaptogen, respiratory and endurance tonic, immune-modulating
- **Evidence:** Traditional use in Chinese medicine. Theoretical to weakly Moderate for exercise performance, where trials are small, brief and frequently industry-funded.
- **Preparations:** fruiting-body extract, commonly one to three grams daily of dried equivalent; note that many products supply mycelium on grain rather than fruiting body
- **Cautions:** immune-modulating, so caution alongside immunosuppressants and in autoimmune disease. Mild antiplatelet activity reported.

### Reishi – Ganoderma lucidum

- **Actions:** immune-modulating, mildly sedating, hepatoprotective in tradition
- **Evidence:** Traditional use, long and well documented. Theoretical for the immune and oncological mechanisms, which rest largely on laboratory and animal work.
- **Preparations:** hot-water extract of the fruiting body, commonly one to three grams daily of dried equivalent; the active polysaccharides require decoction rather than a cold infusion
- **Cautions:** mild antiplatelet activity, so caution with anticoagulants and before surgery. Caution alongside immunosuppressants. Occasional gastrointestinal upset and dry mouth.

## Nervines and sleep

### Passionflower – Passiflora incarnata

- **Actions:** anxiolytic, mildly sedating, antispasmodic
- **Evidence:** Moderate for anxiety, including one small trial comparing favourably with oxazepam. Traditional use for restlessness and sleep onset.
- **Preparations:** infusion of the aerial parts, tincture or extract commonly 250 to 500 mg before bed
- **Cautions:** additive with sedatives, benzodiazepines and alcohol. Avoid in pregnancy. Sedating enough to impair driving in some.

### Lemon balm – Melissa officinalis

- **Actions:** nervine, anxiolytic, carminative, mildly antiviral topically
- **Evidence:** Moderate for anxiety and sleep quality in small trials, frequently in combination products. Traditional use for nervous dyspepsia and for herpes simplex applied topically.
- **Preparations:** infusion of fresh or dried leaf, tincture or extract commonly 300 to 600 mg daily
- **Cautions:** antithyrotropic activity has been shown in laboratory work, so caution in treated hypothyroidism. Additive with sedatives. Generally very well tolerated.

### Skullcap – Scutellaria lateriflora

- **Actions:** nervine, anxiolytic, mildly antispasmodic
- **Evidence:** Traditional use in Western herbalism. Theoretical for the mechanism; the human evidence is thin.
- **Preparations:** infusion of the aerial parts or tincture; dried herb commonly one to two grams per dose
- **Cautions:** historically adulterated with germander, which is hepatotoxic, making verified sourcing more than a preference here. Additive sedation.

### Valerian – Valeriana officinalis

- **Actions:** sedative, anxiolytic, antispasmodic
- **Evidence:** Moderate but genuinely mixed for insomnia. Several trials show benefit, several show none and meta-analyses disagree. Traditional use is long and consistent, which is worth naming alongside the mixed trial picture rather than instead of it.
- **Preparations:** root extract commonly 300 to 600 mg one hour before bed, or a tincture at an equivalent dose; benefit frequently requires two weeks of continuous use rather than a single night
- **Cautions:** additive with sedatives, benzodiazepines and alcohol. Paradoxical stimulation occurs in a minority. Morning grogginess at higher doses. Taper rather than stop abruptly after prolonged use.

### Magnolia bark – Magnolia officinalis

- **Actions:** anxiolytic, sedating, cortisol-modulating
- **Evidence:** Traditional use in Chinese medicine. Theoretical to weakly Moderate for anxiety and sleep, resting on small combination trials and mechanistic work on honokiol and magnolol.
- **Preparations:** bark extract standardised to honokiol and magnolol, commonly 200 to 400 mg in the evening
- **Cautions:** additive with sedatives and alcohol. Avoid in pregnancy. Limited long-term safety data.

### Chamomile – Matricaria recutita

- **Actions:** nervine, carminative, antispasmodic, mildly anti-inflammatory
- **Evidence:** Moderate for generalised anxiety disorder on the strength of controlled trials of standardised extract. Traditional use for digestive upset, colic and sleep.
- **Preparations:** strong infusion of the flowers, covered while steeping to retain the volatile oil; or extract commonly 500 to 1,500 mg daily
- **Cautions:** Asteraceae family allergy, so caution where ragweed sensitivity exists. Mild anticoagulant activity at high intake. Additive sedation.

## Bitters and digestive

### Gentian – Gentiana lutea

- **Actions:** bitter tonic, stimulates gastric secretion and bile flow, appetite-stimulating
- **Evidence:** Traditional use, and the archetype of that category. Theoretical for the secretory mechanism, which is well described but lightly trialled.
- **Preparations:** tincture in drop doses, 10 to 20 minutes before meals; the bitterness on the tongue is the mechanism, so capsules defeat the purpose
- **Cautions:** contraindicated in active peptic ulcer and in gastro-oesophageal reflux, where increased acid secretion worsens the picture. Avoid in pregnancy.

### Artichoke leaf – Cynara scolymus

- **Actions:** choleretic, hepatoprotective, lipid-modulating, digestive
- **Evidence:** Moderate for functional dyspepsia and for modest lipid reduction across several controlled trials. Traditional use as a liver and bile tonic.
- **Preparations:** leaf extract commonly 320 to 640 mg with meals
- **Cautions:** contraindicated in biliary obstruction and in gallstones large enough to obstruct. Asteraceae allergy.

### Dandelion root – Taraxacum officinale

- **Actions:** bitter, choleretic, mild diuretic, hepatic support
- **Evidence:** Traditional use, long and consistent across Western herbalism. Theoretical for the diuretic and hepatic mechanisms.
- **Preparations:** root decoction, roasted root as a beverage or tincture; leaf is the diuretic part, root the bitter and hepatic part
- **Cautions:** biliary obstruction. Asteraceae allergy. The leaf is potassium-rich, which is favourable alongside conventional diuretics but relevant in renal impairment.

### Milk thistle – Silybum marianum

- **Actions:** hepatoprotective, antioxidant, hepatocyte-regenerative
- **Evidence:** Moderate and mixed for chronic liver disease, where trial quality has improved slowly and results remain inconsistent. Strong for amanita mushroom poisoning as intravenous silibinin, which is a hospital intervention rather than anything relevant to a supplement.
- **Preparations:** seed extract standardised to roughly 80% silymarin, commonly 200 to 400 mg daily; poorly water-soluble, so infusions are close to useless
- **Cautions:** Asteraceae allergy. Mild cytochrome effects reported and mostly clinically unimportant. Loose stool at higher doses.

### Ginger – Zingiber officinale

- **Actions:** antiemetic, carminative, prokinetic, anti-inflammatory, mildly antiplatelet
- **Evidence:** Strong for nausea – pregnancy, postoperative and chemotherapy-induced – with consistent meta-analytic support. Moderate for osteoarthritis pain and for dysmenorrhoea. Traditional use for the wider digestive and warming indications.
- **Preparations:** fresh root as decoction or food, dried powder commonly one to two grams daily in divided doses or standardised extract
- **Cautions:** antiplatelet at higher doses, so caution alongside anticoagulants and stop before surgery. Reflux and heartburn in some. Doses above roughly one gram daily in pregnancy exceed what the trials studied.

### Peppermint oil – Mentha piperita

- **Actions:** antispasmodic on smooth muscle, carminative, mildly analgesic
- **Evidence:** Strong for irritable bowel syndrome as enteric-coated oil, with several meta-analyses agreeing on both pain and global symptom benefit. Traditional use for tension headache applied topically in dilution and for general digestive upset as leaf infusion. The Strong grade belongs to the enteric-coated oral preparation and the bowel indication alone – it does not carry to the topical or infusion uses.
- **Preparations:** enteric coating is the whole point for irritable bowel, since uncoated oil releases in the stomach and worsens reflux; commonly 180 to 225 mg of enteric-coated oil two or three times daily before meals
- **Cautions:** worsens gastro-oesophageal reflux and hiatus hernia, markedly so when uncoated. Relaxes the lower oesophageal sphincter. Avoid the oil internally in infants and small children.

### Deglycyrrhizinated licorice – Glycyrrhiza glabra

- **Actions:** mucosal-demulcent, cytoprotective on gastric mucosa
- **Evidence:** Traditional use for gastric and oesophageal irritation, with a modest older trial literature that would grade Moderate at its best and has not been refreshed.
- **Preparations:** chewable deglycyrrhizinated tablets, commonly 380 to 760 mg chewed before meals; the chewing matters, since saliva is part of the mechanism
- **Cautions:** the deglycyrrhizinated form exists precisely to remove the glycyrrhizin. Whole licorice raises blood pressure, depletes potassium and produces a pseudo-aldosterone picture, and it must never be substituted casually for the deglycyrrhizinated form. Whole licorice is contraindicated in hypertension, in heart failure and alongside potassium-losing diuretics or digoxin.

## Anti-inflammatory

### Turmeric – Curcuma longa

- **Actions:** anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, choleretic, mildly antiplatelet
- **Evidence:** Moderate for osteoarthritis pain, where meta-analyses are consistent and effect sizes are meaningful though the trials are small. Moderate for inflammatory markers. Traditional use for the wider Ayurvedic indications. Theoretical for most of what curcumin is sold for, since the in-vitro literature vastly outruns the clinical one.
- **Preparations:** poorly absorbed as raw powder. Standardised curcuminoid extract with a bioavailability system – piperine, phospholipid or micellar – commonly 500 to 1,000 mg daily of curcuminoids
- **Cautions:** antiplatelet activity, so caution with anticoagulants and stop before surgery. Contraindicated in biliary obstruction and caution with gallstones. Reduces non-haem iron absorption. Piperine-enhanced products raise levels of numerous drugs by inhibiting metabolism, which is a real and frequently overlooked interaction. Rare hepatotoxicity reports, most involving high-bioavailability products.

### Boswellia – Boswellia serrata

- **Actions:** anti-inflammatory through leukotriene inhibition, analgesic
- **Evidence:** Moderate for osteoarthritis pain and function across several controlled trials. Traditional use in Ayurveda for inflammatory joint conditions.
- **Preparations:** gum resin extract standardised to boswellic acids, commonly 300 to 500 mg two or three times daily
- **Cautions:** gastrointestinal upset, reflux and loose stool are the common complaints. Theoretical interaction with drugs metabolised through the same pathways. Avoid in pregnancy.

## Immune

### Echinacea – Echinacea purpurea

- **Actions:** immune-stimulating, mildly antiviral, lymphatic
- **Evidence:** Moderate and mixed for reducing the duration of the common cold, with meta-analyses landing on a small effect and considerable heterogeneity between preparations. Traditional use in North American herbalism, where the plant part and species vary meaningfully from what the trials used.
- **Preparations:** fresh-plant tincture or standardised extract, started at the first symptom and dosed frequently for the first 48 hours rather than continuously
- **Cautions:** immune-stimulating, so caution in autoimmune disease and alongside immunosuppressants. Asteraceae allergy, occasionally severe in the ragweed-sensitive. Not for continuous long-term use.

### Elderberry – Sambucus nigra

- **Actions:** antiviral, diaphoretic, immune-supportive
- **Evidence:** Moderate for reducing influenza symptom duration on the strength of several small trials, some industry-funded. Traditional use across European herbalism, extensive.
- **Preparations:** cooked berry syrup or standardised extract, commonly started at first symptom
- **Cautions:** raw berries, leaves, bark and seeds are cyanogenic and must be cooked. Theoretical concern about cytokine stimulation in acute inflammatory illness is frequently repeated and poorly evidenced, and it is named here as theoretical rather than avoided as fact.

### Astragalus – Astragalus membranaceus

- **Actions:** immune-modulating, adaptogenic, tonifying in the Chinese sense
- **Evidence:** Traditional use, long and central to Chinese herbal practice. Theoretical for the immune mechanisms, with the oncology-adjacent trial literature too weak to grade higher.
- **Preparations:** root decoction or extract, commonly three to six grams daily of dried root equivalent; traditionally used preventively rather than during acute infection
- **Cautions:** caution alongside immunosuppressants, including after transplant. Traditionally avoided during acute febrile infection, which is a use rule rather than a safety one.

## Metabolic, cardiovascular and cognitive

### Berberine – Berberis and Coptis species

- **Actions:** glycaemic-lowering, lipid-modulating, antimicrobial in the gut lumen
- **Evidence:** Strong for glycaemic control, with multiple randomised controlled trials and meta-analyses showing effect sizes approaching metformin in type 2 diabetes. Moderate for lipids. Traditional use across Chinese and Ayurvedic practice for dysentery and gut infection.
- **Preparations:** commonly 500 mg two or three times daily with meals; poor oral bioavailability, which is why the dose is high and divided
- **Cautions:** the most interaction-heavy botanical in common use. Inhibits cytochrome P450 3A4 and P-glycoprotein, raising levels of numerous drugs including ciclosporin, statins and many others. Additive hypoglycaemia with diabetes medication. Contraindicated in pregnancy, in lactation and in neonates, where it displaces bilirubin and risks kernicterus. Gastrointestinal upset is common at full dose. Antimicrobial in the gut, so long continuous use warrants attention to the microbiome.

### Hawthorn – Crataegus species

- **Actions:** cardiotonic, mildly inotropic, coronary vasodilating, antioxidant
- **Evidence:** Moderate for symptoms of mild chronic heart failure, with a supportive meta-analysis and one large trial showing no mortality benefit. Traditional use as the central Western cardiac tonic.
- **Preparations:** leaf and flower extract standardised to flavonoids or oligomeric procyanidins, commonly 300 to 900 mg daily; benefit builds over six to eight weeks
- **Cautions:** additive with digoxin, beta blockers, nitrates and antihypertensives. Never a substitute for cardiac care, and any cardiac symptom is a red-flag question before it is a botanical one.

### Bacopa – Bacopa monnieri

- **Actions:** nootropic, memory-consolidating, anxiolytic, adaptogenic
- **Evidence:** Moderate for memory acquisition and retention in trials of 12 weeks or longer, which is the consistent finding across a modest literature. Traditional use in Ayurveda as a medhya rasayana.
- **Preparations:** extract standardised to bacosides, commonly 300 mg daily taken with food; effect requires eight to twelve weeks and is absent acutely
- **Cautions:** cholinergic, so additive with cholinergic drugs and antagonistic to anticholinergics. Gastrointestinal upset and cramping are common and largely avoided by dosing with food. May slow thyroid hormone clearance. Sedating in some.

### Lion's Mane – Hericium erinaceus

- **Actions:** nerve growth factor stimulation in laboratory models, cognitive-supportive, gastric mucosal support in tradition
- **Evidence:** Theoretical for the neurotrophic mechanism, which rests on cell and animal work. Weakly Moderate for mild cognitive impairment on the strength of one small Japanese trial that has not been well replicated. Traditional use in Chinese medicine for gastric complaints.
- **Preparations:** fruiting-body extract, commonly 500 to 3,000 mg daily of dried equivalent; mycelium-on-grain products are largely starch and are not equivalent
- **Cautions:** mild antiplatelet activity reported. Occasional dermatitis and respiratory sensitivity, more common in those with mushroom allergy. Long-term safety data are limited.

### Saint John's Wort – Hypericum perforatum

- **Actions:** antidepressant, anxiolytic, antiviral, vulnerary applied topically
- **Evidence:** Strong for mild to moderate depression, where the trial literature is large and comparable with standard antidepressants. Not established for severe depression. Traditional use for nerve pain and wound healing.
- **Preparations:** extract standardised to hypericin or hyperforin, commonly 300 mg three times daily; four to six weeks to full effect
- **Cautions:** this plant is the archetype of the herb-drug interaction and is included here as much for that reason as for its use. Potent inducer of cytochrome P450 3A4 and P-glycoprotein, which reduces the levels and the effect of oral contraceptives, warfarin, ciclosporin, tacrolimus, antiretrovirals, many statins, digoxin, several anticonvulsants and a long list beyond. Contraceptive failure and transplant rejection are both documented outcomes. Serotonin syndrome risk alongside selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, triptans and other serotonergic drugs. Photosensitivity at higher doses. Any depression severe enough to consider treating is a conversation with a physician first, and suicidal ideation is a red flag under the charter, not an indication for a botanical.

### Reading this file

Three habits keep it honest.

Match the grade to the claim, not to the plant. Ginger is Strong for nausea and Traditional use for warming a cold constitution, and saying so precisely is what the charter means by evidence honesty.

Prefer the lowest sufficient intervention. A bitter before meals is a smaller act than a standardised extract, and the smaller act is tried first where it plausibly suffices, per the ladder.

Check the preparation as carefully as the dose. Enteric-coated against uncoated peppermint, deglycyrrhizinated against whole licorice, fruiting body against mycelium, standardised extract against raw powder – in every one of those pairs the two forms behave differently enough that substituting one for the other changes both the effect and the risk.
