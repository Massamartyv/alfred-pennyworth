Allcon Roofing was built the way the best operations are built – by doing the work properly for long enough that the market stopped checking. What began in 2007 as one founder with a standard now runs residential and commercial divisions across seven states, carries the GAF Master Elite badge held by the top two percent of roofers in the country and backs it with a commercial wall – Sarnafil, Versico, Carlisle SynTec, Johns Manville – that most residential outfits never earn.

This document follows two calls with Jasmine – the first from the studio, the second from Allcon, which is the better direction. She asked for the offer in writing, in enough detail to carry upstairs. This is that document.

The premise is simple. An operation with this certification wall does not have a demand problem. The quieter question – the one no busy operation has time to answer for itself – is what the system between the phone ringing and the money banking actually does all day. Most companies run on systems built for the company they used to be: habits that were craft at two crews and one county, carrying a seven-state operation from memory. The gap between those two companies is the most expensive line on a ledger nobody keeps.

The Allcon homepage puts it better than most consultancies manage: “If you think hiring a good roofer is expensive, try hiring a bad one.” The same arithmetic governs the system behind the trucks. A leaking operation is the most expensive thing on the books, precisely because it never appears there.

The audit exists to put it on paper – in the Allcon numbers, not in theory. What follows is the whole of it: what it is, what it costs and what it opens onto.

01

The discovery · 45 minutes

One sitting with the principals and whoever holds the numbers. The company mapped as it actually runs: where leads come from and what each one costs, how sits become closes, where the office hours go, where the cash cycle drags. Nothing to prepare beforehand and no homework after.

02

The document · built studio-side

The studio builds the audit alone, between meetings. Money leaks first – pricing, close rate, missed and mishandled calls, the commercial cash cycle. Hours leaks second – a task inventory of the office, the double entry, the hand-offs between office and field. No one at Allcon lifts a finger.

03

The presentation · the plan

The document walked through page by page, with an implementation plan ranked by return: what to fix first, what each fix costs and what it costs to leave alone. Every claim in it traces to an Allcon number, so the room can argue with it – and the argument is the point.

The document is yours either way. So is everything it finds.

The two prices

What no costs,

The price of no

Across the trade, a roofing lead trades between $300 and $600 before anyone climbs a ladder. Most companies close one sit in four, and nobody inside the building can say where the other three went. One point of close rate, at roofing ticket sizes, is six figures a year. Whether any of that is true of Allcon is exactly what the audit answers – found in the Allcon numbers, not argued from benchmarks.

None of it lands on an invoice. A storm-week call that rings out, a sit that never got a second touch, an estimate that quietly aged past its moment – the operation pays for each in full, and no ledger anywhere records the payment.

The price of yes

$1,970, once.

No retainer, no contract, no percentage of anything. Two meetings, one document, and the document is Allcon property from the day it changes hands.

The stake is $1,970. The return it is priced against is the close-rate arithmetic above – found once, kept every year after.

The anchors

Roofing peer groups charge $1,200 to $4,800 a month for a version of this attention. A full-time business development hire runs $35,000 to $75,000 a year before commission. This is one number, once, and the finding outlives the engagement.

The exit arithmetic

Private equity is paying four and five times earnings for roofing companies right now. On that arithmetic, every dollar of profit the audit recovers is not a dollar – it is four or five, the day an exit is ever taken. An audited operation is worth more whether or not it ever sells.

What the plan
opens onto

The audit stands alone. It is scoped to justify itself whether or not another dollar ever follows it, and the decision it feeds is made with the document in hand, not before. A plan names fixes, and every fix takes one of three routes: built by Allcon, built by the studio or left alone on purpose. Where the studio builds, the work takes shapes like these.

01

Missed-call capture

Every ring answered, day and after-hours alike – captured, qualified and booked to the calendar without a human standing over it. In storm season, this one tends to pay for the audit by itself.

02

Sit follow-through

The other three sits, chased by system rather than by memory – every estimate followed to a yes, a no or a named reason, so close rate stops being a mystery.

03

The maintenance book

Commercial preventative maintenance renewing itself on schedule – the quietest revenue in the trade, compounding instead of lapsing.

04

The commercial cash cycle

Invoices that chase themselves, so the money arrives while the job is still warm and the yard is not financing the general contractor.
