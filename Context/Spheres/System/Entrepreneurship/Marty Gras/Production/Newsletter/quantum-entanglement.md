# Quantum Entanglement

2026 had barely been born when I learned that my ex-girlfriend had gotten engaged. It comes as no surprise to the version of me that exists today that I was genuinely happy for her.

She was my college sweetheart, my first real love, the relationship that taught me what love had the potential to become. We spent three years building what I believed would endure forever. When it did not, the pain felt like proof that a wound that significant would never heal. Yet here I am, years later, toasting her engagement from my home like some sort of emotionally enlightened person.

They say time heals all wounds. Apparently it also makes you capable of feeling joy for someone else's happiness without needing to treat the success of their new relationship as evidence of your inadequacy, as if love were a competition you lost rather than an experience you lived.

A couple of weeks before this engagement, my most recent girlfriend broke up with me.

And suddenly, I was not so emotionally evolved anymore.

She is a woman I adore in more ways than language has the capacity to describe. Someone I would say I think the world of, if that phrase came anywhere close to the scale at which she should be measured.

We were one of those couples who should not have worked on paper but did in practice. Different life stages. Different priorities. Different everything, really. Except, somehow, we were the same. She understood me in a way that felt cosmic, as if we had both been issued the same manual for being human that everyone else seemed to be missing. We built a world together. Inside jokes that would take twenty minutes to explain to anyone else. A choreographed dance to a song no one else could hear unless they listened closely to the rhythm of our hearts.

Losing her felt less like ending a relationship and more like abandoning an entire world we had been building together. The love I felt for her was grand in a way I am still trying to find the words for.

I have not spoken to her in a month. No calls. No texts. No accidental run-ins at our favorite places, because I have been strategically avoiding them like an active crime scene where I am the prime suspect.

This is what people call no-contact. What it actually feels like is something I can only describe through a thought experiment I half-remember from a science class I took to fulfill a requirement.

Schrödinger's cat. A cat in a box. A fifty percent chance it is dead. A fifty percent chance it is alive. Until someone opens the box, the cat exists in both states simultaneously. Alive and dead. Happy and sad. Together and apart. The box stays closed, and everything remains possible.

Which got me thinking: is falling in love just quantum physics with better branding?

Because here is the thing about no-contact that no one tells you. Every possibility exists at the same time. She might change her mind. She might not. I might move on. I might not. We might reconcile. We might not. The future where she realizes what we had and the future where I realize it is over are both real and both fake, until one of us opens the box and forces reality to collapse into something singular. And honestly? There is something almost comforting about living in quantum superposition. As long as the box stays closed, I get to exist in every version of this story at once.

The difference between the two relationships is that with the first, the box has already been opened. Time forced my hand. Grief did its work. By the time she got engaged, I had already collapsed into acceptance. But with the second, the box is still sealed. She ended it, which means she holds the power to reopen it or leave it closed. I am not in control of the observation. I can only wait in quantum uncertainty, this romantic superposition, or choose to walk away from the box entirely. And I am not sure I know how to do that yet.

I got a cat a couple of months before my last relationship. He became part of the world we built together, another thread in the fabric of our shared narrative. During our relationship, I had to give him up. I took him to the shelter on a Tuesday afternoon and never looked back, never called to check on him afterward. I told myself it was because I did not want to know.

As long as I never called, he existed in every possible state. Adopted by a loving family. Living his best life on a sun-drenched window perch. Or not. The uncertainty felt safer than confirmation. The pain of not knowing was easier to carry than the pain of knowing. Maybe this is karma for choosing comfort over truth.

Sitting at home now, listening to Giveon sing about heartbreak anniversaries, I realize I have been here before.

The box left unopened remains everything.

Which is why we do it. Why I did not call the shelter. Why I have not called my ex-girlfriend. Why I keep building my hopes up like it is my dream job.

I started writing this essay the way I once wrote a song about my college sweetheart. Not because I thought it would fix anything, but because writing has become the way I make sense of things that do not make sense. Back then, I stumbled into catharsis without realizing songwriting could be medicine.

That song ended up resonating with other people moving through their own heartbreaks, which taught me something about how personal grief becomes universal the moment you are brave enough to be honest about it. This time I am being more intentional. Writing through the grief as it happens. Sharing the process publicly. This essay is the evidence. With this breakup came the familiar paralysis of not wanting to do anything except think about her.

Our lives are entangled whether we want them to be or not. The emotions Giveon captures land because he understands that sometimes you are not the observer in the experiment. Sometimes you are the cat. And the person who put you in the box might never come back, might leave you suspended between alive and dead, between loved and forgotten, between reconciliation and abandonment. It is that very uncertainty that gives love its tension, its dangerous power to become heartbreak.

Each day is a gift. Love is a gift best opened and shared. The more gifts you open, the more souvenirs you accumulate, reminders of what is gone when it inevitably leaves. Not through malice, but simply because when it is all said and done, it is done. Death is a symptom of life. The moment you entered this world, you became a player in the box.

Giveon understands that forever and never are both illusions we construct to make sense of uncertainty. Never cannot exist in a world where there is, or was. Forever sounds good in theory, but the only thing that lasts that long is nothing. Death gives meaning to time, not because endings are beautiful, but because they force us to stop living in quantum states and collapse into a singular reality. Opening the box means making decisions that cut off certain possibilities.

I would never see being with her as choosing a lesser life, because she was the life I wanted most. Maybe that dream is dead in the box. Maybe it breathes. Maybe she will open it and show me. Or maybe I will stop giving her that power. Maybe I will walk away from the box and build something new, even if it means never knowing what was inside.

Or maybe I will live here a while longer, suspended between what was and what could be, where everything remains possible, where I love her just as fiercely as I did when we were together. There is a strange beauty in refusing to force reality into something definitive. Maybe some love is meant to be everything, forever.

And sometimes, hope is the only gift worth keeping wrapped.
