# Edward Tufte

**Who** – American statistician and professor emeritus at Yale, where he taught political science, statistics and computer science. The founding figure of modern information design, and his own publisher: he set up Graphics Press and self-published the books rather than accept a printer who would not hold the standard, which is the first thing to know about him. Four principal works – *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information* (1983), *Envisioning Information* (1990), *Visual Explanations* (1997) and *Beautiful Evidence* (2006).

**Why aligned** – He is the case that beauty and function are not in tension, made in the most hostile possible territory. A statistical chart has no licence to be decorative and nothing to hide behind, so every aesthetic decision has to justify itself as an aid to comprehension or be removed. That is the same argument as *caring about how things look does not make you vain* and *the difference between art and aesthetic*, run on data instead of on taste. His vocabulary is a set of judgements with names: **chartjunk** for ornament that displaces information, and the **data-ink ratio** for the proportion of marks on the page actually carrying meaning. He also invented the **sparkline** – a chart small enough to sit inside a sentence.

**The formula** – Remove everything that is not evidence, then make what remains dense. He argues against the instinct to simplify by subtracting data, holding instead that high information density respects the reader; the thing to strip is decoration, not substance. And he treats presentation as an ethical matter rather than a cosmetic one – *Visual Explanations* reads the Challenger launch decision as a failure of evidence presentation, where the charts put in front of the decision-makers obscured the relationship between temperature and O-ring failure. Bad design there was not ugly, it was fatal.

**Canon** – *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*; chartjunk and the data-ink ratio; the sparkline; the Challenger analysis in *Visual Explanations*; his championing of Charles Joseph Minard's 1869 chart of Napoleon's Russian campaign, which he has called possibly the best statistical graphic ever drawn.
