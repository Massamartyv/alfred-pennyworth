# Benjamin Banneker

**Who** – Free Black American mathematician, astronomer and almanac author, 1731 to 1806, born and resident in Baltimore County, Maryland. Largely self-taught. Calculated his own ephemerides and published annual almanacs from 1792 to 1797, running to roughly 28 editions across several printers. Assisted Major Andrew Ellicott in the 1791 survey of the federal territory that became Washington DC.

Two boundaries worth holding, because both errors are common. The persistent story that he reconstructed L'Enfant's plans for the capital from memory is apocryphal – he worked on the boundary survey, and had left the party before L'Enfant departed. The wooden striking clock he is said to have built around 1753 after studying a pocket watch is traditional rather than documented, though it is reported consistently and early.

**Why aligned** – He is the cleanest instance in this library of the work itself being the argument. In August 1791 he sent a manuscript copy of his almanac to Thomas Jefferson, then Secretary of State, with a letter that confronted Jefferson directly on slavery and on the claims about Black intellectual capacity Jefferson had published in *Notes on the State of Virginia*. He did not assert that the claim was false. He enclosed a year of astronomical calculations that could be checked against the sky, and let the enclosure do the work. Then he published the exchange.

There is a second reason he earns a card on 4 August 2026 rather than any other date. Banneker published almanacs in the Franklin tradition. *Poor Charlie's Almanack*, adopted across this system today, is Munger writing in the Franklin tradition. The Mathematics sphere was founded this morning because the arithmetic of uncertainty was the largest gap in the latticework. Banneker sits precisely at the junction of all three, and he got there first.

**The formula** – Do the work to a standard that can be independently verified, then present the work rather than the claim. Verifiability is the whole point: an ephemeris is either right or wrong against the sky, and an argument that can be checked cannot be waved away on the grounds of who made it.

Second: choose the popular form. The almanac was the most widely owned book in an American household after the Bible. He put rigorous astronomy inside the vernacular object rather than into a learned journal, which is the high-low collapse this library already treats as law, executed in 1792.

Third: publish the correspondence. The exchange with Jefferson was itself an artefact, and releasing it turned a private letter into a public document that Jefferson could no longer control.

**Canon** – The 1792 *Pennsylvania, Delaware, Maryland and Virginia Almanack and Ephemeris*, the first and most consequential. The letter to Thomas Jefferson of 19 August 1791 and Jefferson's reply of 30 August, published together in 1792. His astronomical journal, including the solar eclipse calculations in which he found and corrected errors in the published authorities he was working from. The 1791 federal boundary survey under Ellicott.
