# California Bungalow

**What** – The Craftsman-era vernacular of Southern California: one-story or story-and-a-half houses with low-pitched roofs, developed at the turn of the 20th century from Arts and Crafts ideals. The form descends from the "dak bungalows" of 19th-century India – verandaed rest houses for travellers – and reached its zenith under Charles and Henry Greene (Greene and Greene, Pasadena, est. 1894), who fused Mission forms, the Shingle Style and Japanese joinery a decade before Arts and Crafts otherwise reached the West Coast.

**Why aligned** – The dwelling as handcraft. Low horizontal mass settled into its landscape, porches as rooms, structure expressed as ornament – the bungalow is what happens when a house is built the way a piece of furniture is made. The Japanese joinery thread ties it to the same craft reverence the library holds elsewhere; the porch-and-eave logic is early American indoor-outdoor living.

**The filter** – Wide projecting eaves with exposed rafters; heavy square porch columns on stone bases; interiors and exteriors interpenetrating; local materials worked by hand; joinery elaborate enough to be the decoration. Pull it for groundedness – the house that hugs its site rather than posing on it – and for craft made visible at every scale.

**Canon** – the Gamble House, Pasadena (1908-1909), Greene and Greene's "ultimate bungalow" and a National Historic Landmark, built for the Procter and Gamble family and gifted to Pasadena in 1966.

**Pull for** – craftsman, joinery, horizontal-massing, indoor-outdoor, materiality.
